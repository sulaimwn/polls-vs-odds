How it works

How the odds are calculated

Each chamber's headline number is a weighted average of three separate reads on the race: our polling model, Polymarket and Kalshi. Here's the actual arithmetic behind today's numbers.

The polling model, step by step
  1. Collect every poll. All general-election polls of each Senate race and the national generic ballot, from VoteHub. When a firm publishes likely-voter, registered-voter and all-adult versions of one survey, only the likely-voter version is kept.
  2. Weight each poll. Newer polls count more (a poll's weight halves every 3 weeks in Senate races, 2 weeks nationally). Bigger samples count more. Likely voters beat registered voters, who beat all adults. A 3-star pollster counts about twice as much as a half-star one. Repeat polls from the same firm count less so no firm can flood the average.
  3. Correct for sponsors. Polls paid for by a party or allied group count half and are shifted 2 points against the sponsor, because sponsored polls tend to flatter their side.
  4. Average. The weighted polls give each race a margin, such as a 3-point lead.
  5. Add a baseline. Each state also gets a starting guess: its 2024 presidential result, shifted by how much the national generic ballot has moved since 2024, plus about 2.5 points for an incumbent. With lots of polls the baseline barely matters; with none, it's all we have.
  6. Turn the margin into odds. Polls miss. State polls have historically been off by 4–5 points, and more when the election is weeks away, so a 3-point lead works out to roughly a 70% chance, not a sure thing.
  7. Simulate the chamber. The Senate is played out 20,000 times. Each run draws one national polling error shared by every race (misses tend to go the same direction everywhere) plus a smaller error for each state. The polling model's Senate number is the share of runs Democrats win. For the House, the generic-ballot average converts to seats at about 4.5 seats per point.
Why blend polls with markets?

Polls measure voters directly and show their work, which is why they get half the weight. But they're snapshots with shared blind spots. In 2016 and 2020 most polls underestimated Republicans by several points at once.

Markets react in minutes to debates, scandals, early-vote data and anything else traders think the polls are missing, and people bet real money on being right. But a few large traders can move a thin market, and long shots tend to trade above their real odds.

Averaging the two cancels part of each one's errors, the same reason forecasters average many polls instead of trusting one.

Why 50 / 25 / 25?

The polls get half because they're the only direct measurement of voters. Markets share the other half equally: Kalshi is a U.S.-regulated exchange, Polymarket a global crypto market, and they draw different traders. Neither has a clearly better record, so neither dominates. If a source has no market for a race, its weight moves to the others.

When the sources disagree

That gap is information. Some races have polls and traders far apart. The combined number splits the difference, and every race card shows each source so you can judge for yourself.

Weighting

Who paid for the poll, and can you trust it?

Every poll is tagged by sponsor: R-backed D-backed or Nonpartisan, plus Internal for campaign polls. Sponsored polls count half and get a 2-point correction.

Trust ratings come from 538's final pollster ratings (2024 edition, 0.5–3 stars): Top-rated 2.5+, Solid 1.8+, Mixed 1.0+, Low-rated below 1, or Unrated for newer firms. A 3-star pollster counts almost twice as much as a half-star one.

Prediction markets

Polymarket and Kalshi prices are read as probabilities using the bid/ask midpoint when the spread is tight, and the last trade otherwise. Prices are normalised so each race sums to 100%. Polymarket refreshes live in your browser every minute.

Markets carry fees and a mild long-shot bias, so very small probabilities tend to be overstated.

Fine print

  • Independents (Osborn, Bodnar, Achilles, Bengs) count as non-Republican seats. If they end up deciding control, it's scored as a coin flip.
  • House district odds are Polymarket district prices blended with Kalshi and local polls, then shifted together so the 435 seats add up to the national estimate.
  • Polls and Kalshi refresh on the server every 30 minutes; Polymarket prices refresh in your browser every minute.
